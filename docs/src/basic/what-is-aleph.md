# What is Aleph, Really?

Aleph is a chess engine, the same as Stockfish, Leela Chess Zero, and many others. The game of chess is unsolved for nearly all positions except for a select few positions, such as 7-piece tables of Syzygy or Nalimov, which can tell you with absolute certainty the best move to make and the precise way to win the game. For most chess positions, there is no such easy method. Even non-checkmate puzzles don't fall into the category of solved positions, because you still need to finish the rest of the game without catastrophic blunders, which are always possible. Chess engines serve to bridge the gap by a variety of methods to play as strong of moves as they can. Even most trivial implementations of chess engines, just due to the speed of evaluation, and thereby the number of moves they can look ahead, can beat intermediate players, and simple but well-written engines can beat most titled players. 

## A Brief History of Chess Engines

This section is wholly unnecessary for the rest of the documentation, but I thought it interesting enough to keep around, so here is what I believe is a relatively accurate history of chess engines. I'm obviously simplifying a lot and removing a considerable amount of other, extremely notable and important engines, but this is truly an unending rabbit hole. I do however believe that especially for chess, you can learn a lot by looking at the past. I believe that it truly helps show my thinking with Aleph's architecture. 

### The Earliest Chess "Engines"

The earliest chess engines were not really chess engines, but more like a mechanical solver that could work for a specific set of states. For example, El Ajedrecista was a chess "engine" that could deliver a checkmate with a rook and a king against a lone king every time. While more complicated than mating with a queen, which is often one of the first things a new player learns, it still left a lot to be desired in the way of generalization to all chess positions, which is what modern engines strive towards. 

### CPU Engines

Up until 2017 or so, every chess engine was a "CPU" engine. This means that it parallelized its work across many CPU threads and kept its evaluations of a position hand written by humans, which were meant to be extremely cheap so the engine could process many hundreds of millions of positions per second per thread. Since CPU engines have such "weak" (the average CPU engine evaluator without search is still around 1000 rated, or around 80th percentile) evaluators, they use a search method called minimax to search through the game trees. The general way this works is that white wants to maximize their winning chance, while black wants to maximize theirs. Both can't be true at the same time, so minimax works by minimizing the best score the opponent can get on their move. This property leads to a very nice way to represent how good a position is for a player, which we represent with a single number, and for the most part, when players talk about the "evaluation" of a position, they are referring to this version. A more positive number means better for white, and a more negative number means better for black. However, the main issue with this method is that an evaluation of near zero is very hard to interpret properly, even for grandmasters, as most engines are not programmed for their moves to necessarily be understandable to humans. 

Additionally, it is usually a good coding principle to synchronize resource access across these threads (think of trying to read a paragraph but every second someone swaps the page for a completely different one), Stockfish, still to this day one of the strongest CPU engines, foregoes all synchronization in favor of performing an additional validation step on each thread. This allows for it to scale extremely well for a CPU engine. 

### Deep Blue

One of the more famous chess engines was Deep Blue, made by IBM, their third foray into chess, who defeated World Champion Garry Kasparov, widely considered to be one of the greatest players of all time, in a Classical World Championship-style match. Notably, this was not the first time a chess engine had beaten a grandmaster, nor even was it the first time it had beaten a World Champion, but simply the first time that it had beaten a human in this format. This was the first time that engines were truly better than humans at chess.

### AlphaZero

AlphaZero was a chess engine written by Google DeepMind, as an adjustment to their earlier architecture for the game of Go by AlphaGo and AlphaGo Zero. The "Zero" part of the name signifies that it was trained with no prior knowledge of the strategy in chess. You can think of this similarly to handing someone a rulebook and having them play themselves over and over again. Given enough games, you can learn the game of chess this way, albeit it is impossible for a human. Notably, this was the first real case where interpreting the exact way a chess engine thinks about the game of chess was simply out of the question. Before, with CPU heuristic models, it wasn't extraordinary to be able to understand exactly how it thinks about the game of chess, because every heuristic was written by a human, normally a grandmaster. Deep Blue had three grandmasters write its heuristics. AlphaZero also introduced a new method of searching the possible positions, called Monte-Carlo Tree Search, or MCTS, that is able to be better controlled by a neural network. 

### Modern GPU Engines

Soon after AlphaZero, Leela Chess Zero (Lc0) started as an open source variant. Over the years, they made numerous improvements to the design and it often competes directly with Stockfish, albeit it does still lose on average to it. They improved the time management by adding a `moves-left` output to the network directly, so that the network could directly control the time usage in a given position. An easy way to think about it is that, assuming the exact same board, a lower value will mean more time usage. Eventually, Lc0 switched from the same architecture as AlphaZero, which was a convolution based model (think of blurring an image in a very specific way) to a transformer model, which can accurately allow every square to influence every other square at the same time, where convolutions would only be within a local neighborhood.

### Aleph

While Leela Chess Zero may use a transformer model in their most recent versions, it is notably still something called "self-attention". This means that the transformer looks at the board to update the board. This is the same principle used in modern LLMs like ChatGPT. Where Aleph differs from previous engines is similar to how text-to-image transformers work; there are two different inputs to the model. Aleph has a few layers of self-attention to set up for the novel piece of the architecture, something called "cross-attention", where the two inputs are not the same. Where text-to-image models have text on one side and an image on the other, Aleph uses the board as one input and the list of moves on the other. The key idea is that this allows the board to focus on just scoring the current position; the moves then look at the board to determine how good it is. This allows for the size of the model to be much smaller relatively speaking. Aleph is only around 20 million parameters, whereas Lc0-BT4, their current model, is almost 200 million parameters. 

While I have put a lot of effort into making Aleph as fast as possible, this project was written completely from scratch, with only the barest of references to other engines utilized in the `chess` and `caching` modules. This is much to the contrary of something like Stockfish, where it was based on Glaurung 2.1, an earlier engine, and it also is a much older and well maintained engine, having hundreds of contributors. Even Leela Chess Zero, while it was one of the first engines to utilize a neural network, and therefore had no prior code aside from papers written by DeepMind on AlphaZero, was based heavily on AlphaZero, and its models used the structure laid out by AlphaZero up until 2022 when they began utilizing transformers. They have the contributor base to optimize while adding new features, and as such can be a legitimate contender for the strongest chess engine. I mention this to temper expectations of Aleph when compared to these two, as this is a different application of this technology and so should be used as a proof of concept until further notice.
